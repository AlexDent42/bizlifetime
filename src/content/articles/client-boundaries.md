## Does being easy to work with mean saying yes to every request?

Many small businesses worry that a limit will make them seem difficult. So they absorb an extra revision, answer messages at all hours, or squeeze in work that was never part of the agreement. The customer may be happy in the moment, but the hidden cost lands on your schedule, your margin, and sometimes the quality of the original work.

Boundaries do not need to feel like a wall. Clear expectations help both sides know what has been agreed, what happens next, and how a change can be handled.

## Two useful facts about changing work

- **Changes can affect time, cost, and resources.** The Project Management Institute describes scope creep as adding work without addressing its effect on time, cost, resources, or customer approval. A change can still be a good idea, but it deserves an explicit decision. [PMI: Top Five Causes of Scope Creep](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/top-five-causes-scope-creep-6675)
- **A boundary is easier to apply when the agreement explains the process.** Decide how extra requests will be assessed before one arrives, rather than inventing a rule in a tense conversation.

## Put the agreement where both sides can find it

In a proposal, contract, or welcome note, describe the outcome, deliverables, timing, and what you need from the client. For project work, include the number of review rounds and how approvals happen. For ongoing service, say what is included in the regular fee and what counts as additional work.

Be concrete. “Two rounds of consolidated feedback are included” is clearer than “reasonable revisions.” “The monthly plan includes four email campaigns” gives both sides something specific to refer to. Use wording that matches what you can reliably deliver, and have a qualified professional review contracts when needed.

## Agree on communication expectations

Tell clients which channel to use for routine updates, when you normally respond, and what to do if something is urgent. Do not promise an always-on response if you cannot sustain it. A predictable update schedule can reassure customers while giving you focused time to complete the work.

Also explain what you need from them. If a deadline depends on receiving materials or approval, state that clearly and describe how a delay may affect the delivery date.

## Treat a new request as a decision point

When extra work comes up, acknowledge the request and clarify its impact before starting. Offer choices such as:

- replace an item already in scope;
- add time and provide a revised estimate;
- schedule the request as a separate phase;
- keep the current plan and revisit the idea later.

A calm response might be: “We can add that. It will require another review cycle and move delivery to Thursday. I can confirm the added cost before we proceed.” This keeps the conversation focused on the work rather than on blame.

## Make generosity deliberate

Sometimes a small extra effort is the right choice. Make it consciously, record what changed, and consider whether it should become part of the offer in the future. If you routinely provide something outside the agreement, the issue may be that your scope or pricing needs to be clearer.

## Recap: clarify, discuss, agree

- Define the work, timing, communication, and responsibilities early.
- Explain how additional requests will be evaluated.
- Discuss effects on schedule and price before work begins.
- Make exceptions deliberately and update the agreement when patterns emerge.

The point of a boundary is not to win a negotiation. It is to make good work sustainable and keep both sides clear about what they can expect.
