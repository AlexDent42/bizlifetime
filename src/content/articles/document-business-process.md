Most small businesses have important processes that exist only in someone's head. That may work while the same person is always available, but it makes handoffs harder and increases the chance that a routine task will be missed.

Documentation does not need to be formal. A one-page checklist can be enough.

## Pick a process that repeats

Start with a task that happens regularly and has a clear beginning and end: preparing a quote, onboarding a client, packing an order, or closing out a project. Avoid trying to document the whole business at once.

## Capture the steps as you do them

The next time you perform the task, write down the actions in order. Include the information or materials you need, decisions that commonly come up, and any checks that prevent mistakes.

Use plain language and describe the action. “Check that the delivery address matches the order” is clearer than “verify details.”

## Add the useful context

State who owns the process, when it should happen, and what a completed result looks like. Link to forms or templates rather than copying their contents into several places.

## Test it with a fresh reader

Ask a colleague, contractor, or your future self to follow the guide without verbal help. Where do they pause? What do they interpret differently? Update those parts.

## Keep it current

Add a review date or update the guide whenever the process changes. A short accurate document is more helpful than a detailed guide that no longer matches reality.

The value of documenting a process comes from making real work easier to repeat. Keep the guide focused, test it, and improve it as the business learns.
