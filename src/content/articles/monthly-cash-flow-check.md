Cash flow is about when money arrives and when it leaves. A short monthly review can help you see whether the timing of those movements is likely to create pressure, even when the business appears profitable overall.

## Start with the current cash balance

Record the amount available in business accounts at the start of your review. Keep business and personal finances clearly separated where possible, and use the same date each month so comparisons are useful.

## List expected income by date

Include customer payments that are due, not just invoices you have sent. Note which payments are confirmed and which are uncertain. If a payment is late, follow up politely and update your forecast.

## List upcoming costs

Include payroll, rent, subscriptions, loan payments, taxes, inventory, and irregular expenses you can anticipate. Annual insurance or equipment costs can be easy to forget when looking only at a typical month.

## Watch the gap

Compare expected incoming and outgoing cash week by week. A monthly total can hide a short period when bills are due before customer payments arrive. If you see a gap, you have more options when you know early.

## Keep a simple reserve target

There is no single reserve amount that fits every business. Consider how predictable your income is, how quickly you could reduce costs, and what obligations must be paid even in a slow month. Discuss tax and financing questions with a qualified professional who understands your situation.

This review is not a substitute for accounting advice. It is a practical habit that helps you ask better questions and make decisions with a clearer view of the months ahead.
