It is tempting to judge a customer relationship by revenue alone. But the largest invoice does not always represent the healthiest relationship, and a smaller account may be valuable because it returns regularly and works smoothly with your team.

## Consider the full cost to serve

Think about the time spent on communication, custom requests, revisions, support, and administration. Some extra effort is part of good service. The question is whether the effort is understood, priced, and manageable.

If a relationship regularly requires work outside the original agreement, discuss scope and pricing before frustration builds.

## Look for consistency and fit

Customers who are a good fit tend to understand your offer, share necessary information, and have expectations you can meet. They may return, refer others, or give useful feedback. These signs can matter as much as the value of a single project.

## Notice the effect on the rest of the business

Does this work leave enough capacity for other customers? Does it help your team develop a useful capability, or does it pull attention away from the core offer? A customer can be pleasant and still create a poor operational fit.

## Use evidence rather than one difficult moment

One late payment or tense conversation does not define a relationship. Look for patterns over several months: payment timing, repeat work, margin, support demand, and how often expectations are reset.

## Decide what to change

When a relationship is valuable but difficult, try a clear conversation about boundaries, process, or pricing. When the work no longer fits, plan a respectful transition and give reasonable notice where possible.

The goal is not to label customers as good or bad. It is to understand which relationships support the kind of business you want to keep building.
